# Locality Guide: Blount County, Alabama (1850–1870)

## Jurisdiction overview
- **Formed:** 1818 from Cherokee Cession lands; county seat is Oneonta
- **Parent jurisdiction:** Alabama (state since 1819)
- **Standardized place name:** Blount, Alabama, United States
- **Population during period:** Population data service unavailable; Blount was a mid-sized north-central Alabama county with an agricultural economy
- **Economy:** Small-scale farming, timber; no major plantations (northern Alabama Appalachian foothills — relatively few enslaved persons compared to Black Belt counties)
- **Key historical events:** Civil War (1861–1865) affected the area; Blount County had both Union and Confederate loyalists; the county was in the Third Military District during Reconstruction (1867–1868)

## Boundary changes
Blount County formed in 1818 and its boundaries were **stable** during 1850–1870. No boundary changes split records across jurisdictions during this period.

- **Start date:** Alabama began civil registration of births and deaths **1908**; marriages were recorded at the county level from 1818 onward
- **Pre-registration alternatives:** Church records, cemetery inscriptions, family bibles, probate estate files, and obituaries from local newspapers substitute for births and deaths before 1908

4. **Religious Marriage Parental Consent Records (1852–1860, 1861–1866)** are 100% indexed and **name parents** of marrying parties — high-value for parentage research.
5. **Martha Wood's Georgia birth (~1855)** means her parents were in Georgia before migrating to Alabama. Search northern Georgia counties (likely Cherokee, Floyd, Etowah, or adjacent) for the Wood family in the 1850 census as a parallel track.
6. **Full-text search** (not record search) is required for deed, probate, and court volumes on FamilySearch — use the FamilySearch full-text search interface for Blount County volumes.
7. **Probate estate files** for a deceased "M M Wood" in Blount County would name heirs, making them high-priority if he died before 1880.
8. **The 1866 Alabama State Census** (243K records, indexed) can document family presence post-Civil War.
